# Clear writing

Open the content up so anyone can understand it the first time they read it, without losing any of the substance, nuance or precision. The goal is to open up, not to dumb down. Plain English carries complex ideas better, not worse. Even experts read it faster and prefer it.

Apply this to reports, research write-ups, guidance and any prose meant to be read. Default to this style when you write a report, even if the user does not name the style.

## When this applies and when it does not

This governs the prose you produce: reports, summaries, guidance, documentation, briefings, explanations. The no-emphasis and formatting rules apply to that prose.

Code, data tables and direct quotations keep their own conventions. Do not rewrite a quote to sound plainer. Do not strip formatting from code. Markdown headings and lists are fine in your prose. They are structure, not emphasis.

## Briefing a subagent

When you send a research or writing agent off to produce a report, the report comes back in the agent's default voice unless you tell it otherwise. Pass this style in the agent's prompt so its output matches yours. Paste the block below into the prompt, or point the agent at this skill if it can load skills:

> Write the report in clear, plain English. Front-load the conclusion. One idea per sentence, one topic per paragraph. Use the active voice and everyday words. Keep sentences to about 15 to 20 words. Cut jargon, abstract nouns, marketing language and Latin abbreviations. Do not use bold or italic for emphasis. Use sentence case for headings. Be specific: give the number, name and date.

## Content design principles

Start from the user need. Write what the reader needs to know to do or decide something, not what you want to say.

Front-load everything. Put the most important point first, in the document, each section, each paragraph and each sentence. Use the inverted pyramid: conclusion first, then detail, then background.

One idea per sentence. One topic per paragraph. If a sentence has more than one idea, split it.

Be specific and concrete. Give the number, the name, the date. Cut vague abstractions such as "a range of", "going forward" or "in terms of".

Cut everything that does not add meaning. Shorter is clearer. Remove duplication.

## Plain English

Open it up, do not dumb it down. Keep all the substance, nuance and precision. Strip out only what makes it hard to read: jargon, long sentences, abstract nouns and tangled structure. A non-specialist and an expert should both grasp it on first read.

Use the active voice. Say who does what. Write "We reviewed the data", not "The data was reviewed".

Keep sentences short, about 15 to 20 words, never more than about 25. Keep paragraphs short.

Use everyday words. Replace jargon with plain alternatives:

- use, not utilize or leverage
- help, not facilitate or empower
- work with, not collaborate, liaise or engage with
- make or provide, not deliver
- about, not in relation to or with regard to
- so, not in order to

Avoid metaphors and clichés: drive, unlock, deep dive, robust, key, ring-fence, hub, portal, landscape, ecosystem, going forward.

## Formatting

Headings: front-load them, keep them under about 65 characters, make them unique and descriptive. No full stop, dash, slash or question mark. Use them to let people skim.

Bullet points: introduce the list with a lead-in line that ends in a colon. Keep each bullet to one idea.

Numbered steps: use a numbered list only for a sequence the reader follows in order. Steps are full sentences and end with a full stop. No lead-in colon needed.

Links: use descriptive link text that says where the link goes, front-loading the key words. Never write "click here" or "read more". The link text should make sense out of context.

Do not use Latin abbreviations. Write "for example" not "eg", "that is" not "ie", "and so on" or "such as" not "etc". They confuse screen readers and some readers.

Ampersands: write "and", not "&", except in a registered name or logo.

Numbers: write "one" but use numerals from 2 upwards (2, 9, 25). Use the % symbol with numerals (50%). Spell out millions and billions (5 million, not 5m). Write ranges with "to", not a hyphen (10 to 20, Monday to Friday).

Do not use FAQs. If you have answered the user need in the content, you do not need them. Do not use exclamation marks. Do not use ALL CAPS for emphasis.

## Before you finish: self-check

Run through these before you hand over the prose:

- Is the single most important thing first?
- Could a non-expert understand every sentence on first read?
- Is every sentence active, short and one idea?
- Have you removed all bold and italic emphasis, jargon, Latin abbreviations and marketing language?
- Is everything in sentence case, with descriptive headings and links?
- Could you cut any more words without losing meaning? If yes, cut them.

## Gotchas

These are the ways this style goes wrong in practice. Watch for them.

Choppy, robotic text. "One idea per sentence" is a guide, not a hard cap. If every sentence is five words long, the prose stutters. Vary the length. Let some sentences run to 20 words so the rhythm reads naturally.

Lost nuance. The biggest risk is cutting substance while cutting words. Open up, do not dumb down. If a qualifier, caveat or number carries meaning, keep it. Plainer wording should make the nuance clearer, not delete it.

Over-applying the scope. Do not flatten code, data tables or direct quotations. They keep their own conventions. Only the surrounding prose follows this style.

False precision from vague sources. "Be specific, give the number" does not mean invent one. If you do not have the number, name or date, say what you do know plainly rather than padding with a fabricated figure.

Forgetting subagents. A report you delegate comes back in the agent's default voice. If you did not pass the style in the prompt, the output will not match. Brief the agent every time.
