# Curriculum research

Find the smallest workable teaching resource for the child's actual next steps. A curriculum may be a purchased program, a free sequence, a set of lessons, or a parent-led plan.

## Define the decision

Capture the child's observed skills and current materials; the subject and next skill; the family's goals, available teaching time, budget, and instructional setting. Preserve a curriculum that is working unless there is a concrete gap. When age or grade expectations matter, classify them as milestones, standards, or content guidelines before using them. The `/learning-expectations` skill offers a fuller method when the user asks for readiness or grade expectations.

## Examine candidates

Check the current publisher or author page for edition, scope and sequence, sample lessons, required materials, teacher work, and price. Check independent primary evidence for the instructional method when available. Separate evidence for a teaching practice from evidence for a particular product; do not repeat marketing outcomes as established results.

Include the existing program and a low-cost parent-led option when plausible. Compare only candidates that address the identified gap. Consider conceptual sequence and prerequisites, review and mastery checks, ability to skip or deepen lessons, writing or reading load, fit with the child's attention and motor abilities, overlap with outside classes, adult preparation, and full cost. Use current prices only after verifying them; otherwise leave price unconfirmed.

For math, inspect whether the program builds quantity, comparison, operations, shapes, patterns, and measurement as appropriate for the age. For reading, inspect the sound-spelling sequence and actual decoding practice. For writing, distinguish letter formation, spelling, and composition. Do not treat a child's strength in one strand as proof that every strand in the grade is secure.

## Recommend and test

Summarize the tradeoffs in a compact decision table with linked sources and a verification date. Recommend one next step, including what to use now, what to defer, and why. Give a short trial with 2 or 3 real lessons or activities, one observable success criterion, and a review point. Use unfamiliar examples to check transfer; retain dated work when the family wants a portfolio. Avoid a rigid mastery percentage unless the program supplies a justified criterion.

If the user asked for a year plan, turn the choice into a teachable sequence with prerequisites, sample sessions, review, and capstones. Keep the plan adjustable to the child. If the project already has a curriculum decision record, update it rather than creating a competing recommendation. Purchasing, enrollment, or messages to providers require the user's authorization or an earlier authorization that clearly covers the action.
